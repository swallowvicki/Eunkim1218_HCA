Design system

One system — from tokens to shipped code

Raw color ramps become roles — surface, border, brand, danger. A color decision is made once, then referenced everywhere.

02Components, fully spec’d

Every variant × state in one matrix — default, hover, focus, filled, error, disabled. Nothing is left to a developer’s guess.

03Patterns & templates

Components compose into repeatable page patterns — tables, drawers, wizards — so a new screen starts at 80% done.

041 : 1 with code

The library mirrors engineering’s shadcn setup: tokens land as CSS variables, components land as props. What I design is what ships.

End-to-End Product Design · FashionGo

Net Terms

Bringing Net Terms to FashionGo buyers — a split payment of Net Terms + card, designed so two payment systems never feel like two problems.
Timeline4 months
My roleProduct / UX design — end to end
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformFashionGo · Buyer (mobile)
TL;DR
  • Net Terms rarely pays alone — when the balance falls short, a card covers the rest. But that card runs on a different payment gateway than FashionGo's regular checkout, forcing a second, separate card registration.
  • I split the two card contexts into clearly separated boxes — stored separately at checkout, and registered & managed as two distinct sections in My Account.
  • Revenue grew more than 10% after Net Terms launched.
01 · CONTEXT

Where this started

FashionGo introduced Net Terms — buy now, pay on credit terms — for wholesale buyers. I designed the buyer-side experience, from how Net Terms appears at checkout to how buyers manage the cards behind it.

Checkout · payment method — the starting point (non–Net Terms user, card only)
02 · PROBLEM

The problem

Net Terms is not a standalone method: the real flow is Net Terms + card. When a buyer’s Net Terms balance can’t cover the order, the shortfall is charged to a card. The catch — that card runs on a different PG than the one FashionGo uses for normal card payments, so it must be registered separately. Left as-is, buyers would see two card registrations for “the same thing” and reasonably assume something was broken.

03 · CONSTRAINTS

What made it hard

The two payment gateways cannot share a stored card — separate registration was technically unavoidable. The design question became: how do we keep “two cards” from feeling like a bug?

04 · PROCESS

Exploration & process

The first 3 weeks went into UX/UI structure and branding — getting the shape of Net Terms right before drawing screens. From there it became a continuous loop with the PM and engineers: reviewing what could be built fast and accurately, and adjusting the design to match.

The core edge case — order total exceeds the Net Terms balance
And its sibling — Net Terms chosen, but no backup card stored yet
05 · KEY DECISIONS

Three decisions that shaped the design

Decision 1 — One payment, not an error. Net Terms + card behaves as a single method at checkout: if the balance falls short, the card quietly covers the remainder instead of failing the order.

Balance shortfall handled inside checkout — the card covers the remainder, the order never fails

Decision 2 — Two card contexts, two separate boxes. Each card is saved in its own clearly-labeled box, so buyers always see which card serves which purpose — no silent reuse, no ambiguity.

Two card contexts as two clearly separated boxes at checkout

Decision 3 — My Account mirrors the separation. Card registration and management are split into distinct sections in My Account, matching the checkout structure — each card is registered and managed where buyers expect it.

My Account · Net Terms — registration & management mirror the same separation
06 · COLLABORATION

Working with PM, engineering & stakeholders

The hardest debate was about the vendor side. The PM argued vendors must know an order is a Net Terms order — vendors run Auth & Capture, and if a charge declines, the payment method has to change, so surely they need to know.

I argued from the user’s seat: a vendor cares that an order came in — not which rail it pays on. And structurally, the rails can’t cross: a Net Terms order can only be re-charged through Net Terms (PG: Balance), a card order only through card (PG: Stripe). Since payment methods can never switch between gateways, telling vendors “this is a Net Terms order” adds information they can’t act on.

The argument was accepted — we removed the PG / payment-method display from the vendor’s order detail page entirely.

07 · LAUNCH

Shipping it

Shipped with the simplified vendor order detail: vendors never see which gateway an order runs on — and with nothing to misread, capturing payments actually got easier, not harder.

Figma / live screen · final design
08 · IMPACT

What it changed

+10%revenue growth after launch
—zero payment-method confusion on the vendor side

Net Terms lifted revenue by more than 10%. On the vendor side, removing the gateway display eliminated a whole class of confusion — vendors simply capture, without ever needing to know which PG is underneath.

09 · LEARNINGS

What I'd tell my past self

Auth & Capture belongs to the system, not the vendor. Asking vendors to capture manually is pure burden — the right design captures automatically the moment an order ships.

You can’t change a payment system without shipping. Payment states only move when Ship events move them — integrating the two isn’t optional, it’s the design.

New Feature Launch · FashionGo

Settlement 1.0

From monthly manual invoices to automatic per-transaction settlement — with speed as a product.
Timeline~4 months
My rolePayout & settlement UX · ERP fit
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformFashionGo · VA + WA
TL;DR
  • FashionGo had no settlement concept at all — every month, CS and the Billing team issued invoices by hand and collected or paid out manually.
  • We replaced it with per-transaction settlement on two tracks: free at Shipped + 21 days, or early at Shipped + 2 days for a 3% fee.
  • Vendors now get paid automatically — no more mailing checks to the company. The change landed as instant relief.
01 · CONTEXT

Where this started

FashionGo ran on invoices, not settlement. Each month CS and the Billing team manually issued invoices, chased payments, and paid sellers out — a process whose cost grew with every transaction. Settlement 1.0 replaces it: money settles automatically, per transaction, after a defined holding period.

Vendor Admin · Payment & Settlement — “Standard Payout: Shipped date + 21 days”, with per-transaction settlement rows and Express Payout fees
02 · KEY DECISION

Two payout tracks — the holding period as a product

Why hold money at all? If nothing comes back, the platform could pay out right after shipping. But when a return or dispute arrives, the platform must still be holding the money to refund the buyer. The holding period is protection — and shortening it is a risk someone has to price.

Track 1 — Free. Settle at Shipped + 21 days, no fee: the window covers returns and disputes.

Track 2 — Fast, for 3%. Settle at Shipped + 2 days with a 3% fee: the platform absorbs the return risk itself, and the fee prices that risk. Sellers choose between free-but-patient and fast-but-paid.

03 · MY PART

Making money legible

My focus was the receiving side of that system: making payouts and settlement invoices easy to find, read and trust — and structuring the data so it connects cleanly to ERP systems, since wholesale sellers reconcile everything downstream.

Settlement Statement — release versions compared side by side, itemized down to payout, refund and fee lines
05 · IMPACT

What it changed

—manual monthly invoicing (CS + Billing) replaced by automatic settlement
autopayouts — vendors paid without sending a single check

Going from “no settlement concept” to automatic per-transaction payouts proved right immediately: vendors loved being paid automatically instead of mailing checks, and the monthly CS / Billing invoicing burden disappeared.

Growth / Conversion · FashionGo

Single Vendor Free Shipping

Free shipping buyers can actually reach — funded 50/50, and turned into a sales engine with AI recommendations.
Timeline~4 months
My roleBuyer-side UX · threshold & discovery design
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformFashionGo · Buyer
TL;DR
  • Free shipping funded 50/50 by the vendor and FashionGo, shipped on FG-provided carrier accounts — FG’s negotiated UPS/FedEx rates often beat what a vendor could get alone.
  • I made the benefit — and its $300-per-vendor threshold — impossible to miss, and added AI-recommended items to help buyers close the gap.
  • Vendors using free shipping saw sales rise more than 5%.
01 · CONTEXT

A three-way win

The project gives buyers free shipping on an order — with the cost split 50% vendor, 50% FashionGo. Shipping runs on carrier accounts FG provides: FG negotiated rates with UPS and FedEx that often beat what a vendor could get negotiating alone (the biggest vendors may have better rates of their own). Vendors ship cheaper, FG grows orders, buyers get the benefit — everyone wins.

Shopping Bag — each vendor section carries its own free-shipping eligibility and progress
02 · KEY DECISIONS

Make the benefit — and the threshold — unmissable

Decision 1 — Visibility first. I placed free shipping where buyers can’t miss it, so as many orders as possible actually use the benefit.

Decision 2 — Surface the $300 rule. Free shipping requires $300+ per single vendor. That condition is shown right where buyers build their order, with per-vendor progress — never discovered too late at checkout.

03 · MY IDEA

When the cart falls short, recommend the gap away

If a buyer’s cart doesn’t reach $300, we don’t just show a warning — an AI-powered search recommends items from that vendor so the buyer can fill the gap, unlock free shipping, and the vendor sells more in the same order. Turning the threshold from a wall into a sales engine was my idea.

“Shop more from this vendor” — AI-recommended items appear inside the bag when the cart is below $300
04 · COLLABORATION

Working with PM, engineering & stakeholders

The 50/50 split wasn’t a guess. We pulled the data on what vendors actually spend on shipping, analyzed and compared it, and set the ratio on evidence. Vendors accepted it without pushback — happily.

05 · IMPACT

What it changed

+5%sales growth for vendors using free shipping
50/50vendor / FG cost split — benefit without one side carrying it
0vendor complaints — a data-backed split that landed well

Vendors offering single-vendor free shipping saw their sales rise by more than 5% — the threshold-plus-recommendation loop converts shipping cost into order growth.

Complex Flow · FashionGo

Itemized Return

From whole-order returns to item-level refunds — that still add up correctly.
Timeline~4 months
My roleReturn & refund UX — vendor + buyer side
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformFashionGo · VA + Buyer
TL;DR
  • Returns always meant returning the entire order — Release 1.0 makes returns work per item, inside one order.
  • Vendors enter the restocking fee and refund amount directly; and since one payment spans many charge components, every refund resolves component by component.
  • Showing buyers exactly what gets refunded was my proposal — and it was adopted.
01 · PROBLEM

All or nothing

A return used to be all-or-nothing: the whole order came back, even when only one item was wrong. Itemized Return breaks that apart — buyers and vendors settle a return at the item level, inside a single order sheet.

02 · VENDOR SIDE

Vendors control the numbers

On the vendor’s Return/Refund Details, every line item carries its own return quantity — and the vendor directly enters the restocking fee and the refund amount. The refund summary recomputes as they type, so what the vendor approves is exactly what the money does.

Vendor Admin · Return/Refund Details — per-item return quantities, vendor-entered restocking fee, live refund summary
03 · MY IDEA

Show buyers exactly what comes back

One payment isn’t one number — it spans item amounts, vendor promotions, FG coupons, shipping and handling fees. If a refund happens, it has to resolve against each of those components, and that resolution was the heart of this design. I proposed that the buyer’s order detail should show precisely what is being refunded, line by line — item refund, promotion and coupon adjustments, shipping and handling refunds, restocking fee — and the proposal was adopted.

Buyer · Order Detail — the refund broken down line by line: item refund, promotion & coupon adjustments, shipping / handling refunds, restocking fee
05 · IMPACT

What it changed

—whole-order returns replaced by item-level returns
↓whole-order return rate fell after launch

The share of whole-order returns dropped. With refunds settled at the item level, large sums no longer swing back and forth for a single wrong item — vendors and buyers both came out ahead.

Label It — Relabeling Program

Big buyers rebrand what they buy. I designed the system that makes it happen — across the factory, the buyer and the vendor.
Timeline~4 months
My roleEnd to end — factory · buyer · vendor surfaces
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformFashionGo · Buyer + Factory + VA
TL;DR
  • Big buyers purchase wholesale goods and want them relabeled with their own brand tags — goods go to a sewing factory, get retagged, then ship to the buyer.
  • The flow: buyer pays → the relabeling factory is notified → the factory quotes the total price → relabeling runs → finished goods ship to the buyer.
  • I owned all three surfaces: the system the factory works in, what the buyer sees at payment, and what the vendor sees in their admin — so everyone can follow the flow.
  • My first project after adopting AI — designed on shadcn components, so engineering could take the design straight into code.
01 · CONTEXT

What relabeling is

A big buyer doesn’t just want the goods — they want the goods under their own label. So instead of shipping vendor → buyer, the order routes through a sewing factory: tags are swapped to the buyer’s brand, then everything ships on to the buyer’s address. Turning that side-arrangement into a platform feature meant designing for three different users at once, each needing to see their part of one flow.

02 · THREE SURFACES, ONE FLOW

Factory · Buyer · Vendor

① The factory’s system. A working tool for the relabeling factory: orders arrive by state (New → Dropped Off → Shipped), the factory confirms the total price, and scan-driven actions (“scan for drop off”, “scan for shipping label”) keep the floor moving without paperwork.

Factory system · Label It — orders by state, with scan-for-drop-off and scan-for-shipping-label actions

② What the buyer sees at payment. Relabeling appears right in checkout — buyers opt in and see it as part of their order, not a side deal.

Buyer · Checkout > Shipping — the relabeling service surfaced where the buyer pays

③ What the vendor sees. Vendors follow the same order in their admin — they know it’s routing through relabeling and where it stands, so the flow is never a black box.

Vendor Admin · Order Details — the relabeling order’s state and drop-off routing, visible to the vendor
03 · NEW WAY OF WORKING

First project of the AI era — designed in shadcn

Label It was the first project I ran after adopting AI into my workflow — and I changed how the design itself was built. Instead of drawing free-form screens, I designed on shadcn, the component library engineering actually ships with. Every screen above is composed of components developers already have, so the design could be applied directly in code — no translation layer, no “dev version” drift between what I drew and what shipped.

04 · COLLABORATION

Working with PM, engineering & stakeholders

We started with factory interviews — learning exactly how relabeling runs on their floor today — and designed so the factory never has to abandon or fight its existing process. Shipping labels are provided by FG, so the factory takes no financial hit for joining the program.

05 · IMPACT

What it changed

3user surfaces designed as one flow — factory · buyer · vendor
1:1design → code: shadcn components engineering ships directly
↑big-buyer signups grew after launch

Big-buyer signups increased and revenue grew — relabeling turned a side arrangement into a reason to join the platform.

Flagship · Phase 2 · Shopcord

Vendor Scale-Up + SMS Chat

My largest project — growing the vendor side from a linesheet tool into a real operation, and giving vendors a live, two-way SMS conversation with their buyers.
Timeline4 weeks · 28 build sessions
My roleVendor-side UX — end to end
TeamUX team · PM · engineering
PlatformShopcord · Vendor
TL;DR
  • Scaled the vendor side across linesheets, items, orders and statistics — then added the flagship: SMS Chat, a two-way inbox between vendor and buyer.
  • Designed the full conversation lifecycle — drag-&-drop image composing, email alerts capped at 4/day, and a 90-day archive policy so closed threads resurface instead of disappearing.
  • Carried it to the finish line myself: annotated for Figma handoff, ran QA, shipped to release — then kept it healthy with SCM tickets and weekly reports.
01 · CONTEXT

From tool to operation

Phase 1 gave vendors a linesheet tool. Phase 2 had to make the vendor side a place where a wholesale business actually runs — more linesheets, real orders, live numbers, and a direct line to buyers. Four weeks, twenty-eight build sessions.

02 · THE SCALE-UP

Linesheets, orders, numbers

Linesheets & templates. Template A/B/C with change-template built in, and a compact list layout so vendors with dozens of linesheets can still scan them in one view.

Items & orders. Bulk upload for the catalog, and order creation with a 640px item-detail drawer — the vendor never loses the order they’re building.

Statistics. Period-filtered metrics, separated clearly from all-time values — plus the number vendors care about most: “N buyers have $X waiting in their carts.”

03 · THE FLAGSHIP

SMS Chat — a two-way inbox

Campaigns only talk at buyers; SMS Chat lets vendors talk with them. A real 1:1 inbox: drag-and-drop images straight into the composer, unread states, customer context and campaign history alongside the thread, and new-message email alerts capped at 4 per day so notifications inform instead of spam.

Archive, not amnesia. After 90 days a conversation closes — but when a new thread opens, the old conversations surface as an archive list right beside it. Context survives the policy.

A few more projects

Two smaller projects that don't fit the FashionGo / Shopcord story, but round out the range — an internal ERP/CRM build and an early mortgage-lending redesign.
01 · ERP / CRM

BadaPro

Concept

A web-based ERP + CRM built for a water purifier company, unifying customer records, inventory, and technician scheduling into one system that runs the whole service business.

Concern

Inventory and customer data had to move in lockstep, and each of the five filter types ran on its own replacement cycle. The real problem: the moment a technician swapped a filter and captured the customer's signature, the next visit had to schedule itself automatically — correctly, for whichever filter it was.

Solution

I built inventory around configurable filter cycles, so every filter type generates its own next-visit date the instant a job closes — no manual rescheduling, no missed cycles. Mobile signature capture let technicians close out jobs on-site, and a dedicated technician mode kept the whole flow fast and field-ready.

OUTCOMEOne system, five filter cycles, service visits that schedule themselves
BadaPro — technician scheduling app and admin CRM/inventory dashboard
